Head orientation · foundation for steps 1 and 3

Where is the head pointing?

The IMU beside the lens never reports an angle. It reports how fast the head is turning, about 78 times per second. Adding up those small turns gives the head’s direction. This page shows how, why the answer drifts, and what it costs the processor.

1 · Rate, not angle

Turning speed × time = small turn; add them all up

Each gyro read gives three turning speeds in degrees per second. If two reads are 12.9 ms apart and the head turned at an average 30 °/s between them, it turned 0.387° in that interval. The program keeps a running total. That is integration, and it is only as good as the samples it adds up.

The servo does not turn smoothly: it receives 1° commands every 0.2 s and jumps in short bursts. Below, a model of the real pan test (0 → −20° → +20° → 0) is sampled the way the polling thread samples it. Make the pause longer and watch the estimate stop returning to zero.

3 · Bias

The error that grows while nothing moves

A still gyro does not read exactly zero. At start-up the program averages the readings over a still window and subtracts that bias. Whatever bias remains is added up forever: a leftover 0.05 °/s becomes 3° per minute. In a 60 s still recording the estimate wandered 2.72° in 49.96 s after a 10 s calibration window measured. Use the “leftover gyro bias” slider above to see the ramp it causes.

Why step 3 exists. Integration has no anchor, so error only accumulates. Step 3 corrects it with evidence that does not drift: when ORB recognizes a remembered view, the program knows the head points where it did when that view was stored; the phase-correlation image shift gives a second opinion on each turn.
4 · Two sets of axes

The IMU is mounted at an angle to the camera measured

The IMU board’s x, y, z axes are not the camera’s right, down and forward. The project fitted the relationship from recorded pan and tilt sweeps and stored it in camera_axes_neutral.json. Each row says which IMU direction is a camera direction:

camera axisIMU xIMU yIMU z
right−0.0550.0430.998
down0.1880.982−0.032
forward−0.9810.186−0.062

So camera right ≈ IMU +z, down ≈ IMU +y, forward ≈ IMU −x. The 0.19 entries mean the board is rotated about relative to the lens derived. Each gyro sample is converted with three dot products, camera rate = M · IMU rate, before integration (camera_components()).

5 · Why a quaternion?

In 3D, the order of turns matters illustrative

You cannot keep separate running totals for pan and tilt and add them: a turn about one axis changes the direction of the others. The same two 90° turns in a different order point the camera somewhere else:

A quaternion is four numbers (w, x, y, z) that store a whole 3D orientation and compose turns in the right order. RelativeCameraOrientation.add() does this for every gyro read:

If two reads are more than 0.2 s apart the estimate is marked unknown instead of guessing across the gap. The quaternion is an orientation relative to the still start, not a position or a compass heading.

6 · On the processor

A thread that wakes, reads and sleeps

wake→I2C read ≈2.6 ms→convert axes + integrate→sleep 10 or 50 ms→repeat

The loop is one Python thread. A shorter pause means more reads, more conversions and more Python work. Measured on the Pi (median of two runs each): reads went from 407 to 1,671.5 per 21.5 s window and recorder CPU from 1.96% to 4.04% of one core measured. An adaptive policy (50 ms when still, 10 ms while the servo moves) cut reads by 16%, but the measured recorder CPU did not fall: per-read cost is small next to the fixed overhead of the thread and logging. Fewer operations did not mean less CPU time here.

CPU seconds are processor time spent running the recorder’s instructions, not wall time or energy. Sleeping and waiting on I2C take wall time without necessarily using a core.
